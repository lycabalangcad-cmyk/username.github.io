Why this guide exists

Generative AI tools can explain ideas, give feedback and help you plan. They can also invent facts, hide weak understanding and, if used against the rules, put your grades at risk. Many students are simply unsure where the line is, because rules differ between courses and institutions.

This guide gives you a practical way to think about it. It is general advice, not official policy.

Five principles

1. Know the rules

Read your course outline and your university's AI guidance. If it is unclear or silent, ask your instructor before you rely on AI.

2. Learn, don't outsource

Use AI to understand, practise and get feedback. Be careful about letting it do the thinking you are being assessed on.

3. Verify everything

AI can sound confident and still be wrong. Check facts, numbers, quotations and references against reliable sources.

4. Protect data

Do not enter personal details, other people's information or confidential research into public tools.

5. Be transparent

Disclose AI use whenever it is required, and keep a record of how you used it in case you are asked.

Fine, check first, or not okay?

These examples are typical, not universal. A course can allow or forbid more than shown here.

Table 1. A rough guide to common uses of AI in study
LevelExamples
Usually fineExplaining a concept in simpler words; making practice questions; brainstorming topics you then develop yourself; summarising a paper that you also read.
Check firstGrammar or style help on your own draft; asking for an outline; using AI where the course has no written policy; any use in graded work where disclosure may be required.
Usually not okaySubmitting AI-written work as your own; using AI where the assignment forbids it; copying unchecked references; entering private or confidential data; hiding AI use when disclosure is required.

How to disclose AI use

Where your course asks for it, a short, honest statement is usually enough. You can adapt this template:

I used [tool name and version, if known] on [date] to [purpose, e.g. brainstorm topic ideas / check grammar in my own draft]. I reviewed and edited all output, and the final work is my own.

Some citation styles, such as APA and MLA, have published guidance on citing generative AI. Check the latest edition of the style your course uses, and follow your instructor's instructions if they differ.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting references. AI tools can produce sources that look real but do not exist.
  • Trusting numbers. Statistics and dates must be checked at the original source.
  • Copy and paste. Submitting output you cannot explain is risky, even if rules allow AI.
  • Sharing too much. Pasting personal or confidential material into a public tool.
  • Assuming rules are the same everywhere. What one course allows, another may forbid.
  • No record. Not saving your prompts, so you cannot show how you worked.

About & Feedback

What this site is for, what it is not, and how your data is handled.

Purpose

AI-Smart Study helps university students use generative AI tools responsibly, effectively and transparently. It was created as a student project for a Social Media Analytics course.

What this site does not do

  • It does not run AI models, create accounts or collect your name.
  • It does not give official policy, legal or academic-integrity rulings for any university.
  • It does not help anyone avoid course rules.

Privacy & analytics

This site is static, so the prompt builder, quiz and checklist all run in your browser. Prompt text, quiz answers and checklist ticks are not sent to a server by the site. Checklist ticks and your analytics choice are stored in your browser only.

If analytics is enabled, the site uses Google Analytics 4 to count visits and learn which pages and tools are used (for example how many people copy a prompt or finish the quiz). A banner asks for your choice, and you can change it later from the footer. Google handles this data under its own terms.
